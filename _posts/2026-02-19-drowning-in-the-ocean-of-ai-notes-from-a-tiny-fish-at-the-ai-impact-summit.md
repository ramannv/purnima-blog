On the opening morning of the AI Impact Summit, I arrived at Bharat Mandapam at 9 AM and was greeted by an ocean of people. The chaos of opening day has been well documented by numerous Twitter (X) handles and news outlets. The participants have expressed their dismay online, and I don't want to rehash it. What I want to talk about is what I *felt*. Not as a tech enthusiast, not as a startup founder, but as an AI policy researcher, as someone who strongly believes this technology is moving too fast, and that we have almost no regulatory frameworks in place to catch up.

What follows is an honest account of being overwhelmed in waves.

## **The First Wave: The Sheer Scale of It**

As I tried to navigate the massive complex, my first point of overwhelm was simply *looking* at the number of people. A small aside: the ladies' queue was much, much shorter than the gentlemen's queue. Convenient in the moment, but on reflection, it was a quiet indicator of how few women were present at India's biggest AI summit. Something worth sitting with.

I had come prepared with a carefully curated list of panels I wanted to attend. But the sessions overlapped aggressively, and I, being a single entity with no clones, could only be in one room at a time. The saving grace was that everything was recorded. So now this single entity has to go back and watch it all soon before the urgency fades and the recordings become just another tab I never get to.

I divided my time between attending governance panels, listening to hallway conversations of people trying hard to network, and doing what I do best, observing the crowd. Tech bros. Lawyers. Youngsters building cool stuff. AI startup founders. Government officials. International delegates. School students. Organisers. Security staff. And even families with children picnicking on the lawns outside. You name it, they were there. 

## **The Second Wave: What Is Everyone Doing Here?**

And that was my second wave of overwhelm. What exactly is everyone doing here?

This ocean of people, swimming like tiny fish in the sea. Is everyone this excited about the future of AI? How many of them are actually building AI? How many truly understand the core technology? How many are here simply because they didn't want to suffer FOMO later? Can they distinguish between what is genuinely AI and what is just digital technology slapped with an AI label? (There was a lot of that happening.) Are they also trying to understand what this is all about? Is the hype real, or is it hyped *because* they are all here like a feedback loop of excitement generating more excitement?

In that moment, with dozens of simultaneous questions running as open tabs in my head, a new browser window opened, and it asked: *What the hell am I doing here?*

I felt like an even tinier fish in the ocean. Almost drowning.

## **The Third Wave: So… Why Is Everyone Building AI?**

Let me be honest. I am not overly optimistic about AI being deployed everywhere when the policy infrastructure to govern it barely exists. A few things worry me. What happens to people's jobs? What happens when we start treating AI as a magic fix for deep, messy societal problems instead of actually solving them? What happens when the state has this kind of power, and there's no one watching? But I'm not a Luddite either. I do believe in the transformative power of technology, and there are some genuinely good products being built. The expo grounds had ample evidence of that on showcase.

But the line between what is good and what is harmful is a blur right now. And without a regulatory framework to draw that line clearly, it's a tricky road ahead.

Here's what struck me most: big tech companies are selling the dream that AI is so transformative it will eradicate humanity's problems. And as a ripple effect, new, smaller companies and founders are racing to build *something*, *anything* that claims to have solved a problem. Or a non-problem. Or an imaginary problem.

But why? Is it because this is where all the money is at right now? Is it the romance of being part of a revolution? Or is it the fear that if they don't "adapt," they'll become obsolete?

Somehow, it all comes down to FOMO, doesn't it? The same FOMO that fills this venue with lakhs of people may also be fuelling the entire AI ecosystem. Funny. And a little terrifying.

## **The Fourth Wave: The Rooms Where Governance Happened (Sort Of)**

Since I was there for governance, I made it a point to attend a couple of panels on the topic. If I had to summarise most of the sessions I sat through, it would look like a word cloud, the kind you'd generate if you fed every AI policy speech from the last two years into a blender: 

*AI is reshaping. AI holds promise. Innovation at scale. Regulatory sandboxes. Sovereign AI models. Digital public infrastructure. AI for economic growth. AI as a growth engine. Trustworthy AI. Responsible AI. Ethical AI. Accountable AI.*

You get the idea. The vocabulary of AI governance has become a genre unto itself: familiar enough to sound reassuring, vague enough to mean almost anything. 

And it wasn't just the words. Some of the panels had a representation problem, too. I overheard hallway complaints, *oh, they're just a politician, what do they know about the tech? Why was a lawyer talking in there?*

I have mixed feelings about this. Yes, some panels had poor representation of the topics being discussed. But I strongly disagree that only tech experts and AI startup founders deserve a seat at the table. Some of the most valuable voices in these rooms were professionals from law, education, and health who had done the hard work of genuinely understanding AI and engaging with the technical details, not just the surface. But there are also people on those panels whose understanding of AI begins and ends with using LLMs in their workflow. They are simply amazed that large language models can do so much, and that amazement has become their entire expertise.

To be fair, there was a clear acknowledgement of the need for AI governance. The nexus between industry and regulators is visibly grappling with the pace at which the technology is evolving. You could almost sense them struggling to stay afloat, desperately reaching for something to hold onto. A floaty. The floaty here being some kind of framework: principles, guidelines, standards, whatever, that can keep them from sinking and allow the AI deployment machine to keep running.

And since I keep using the word "governance", let me say what I actually mean by it. I don't mean something abstract or academic. I mean **regulation**. The kind of structured, enforceable oversight we already have for aviation, healthcare, and cybersecurity, or any other sector where the stakes are high enough that we decided we can't just let companies figure it out on their own. AI, increasingly being deployed in governance, defence, healthcare, and education, deserves the same scrutiny. That's all. Not a ban, not a slowdown for the sake of it, but just the basic expectation that a technology this powerful should have rules around how it's built, deployed, and held accountable when things go wrong.

But here's what troubled me: while the talk about governance was audible inside the panel rooms, it was barely a whisper in the hallways. Out there, the dominant chatter was about how AI is awesome, a goldmine, a problem solver, the next big revolution. The gap between the conversations inside and outside those rooms was its own kind of overwhelm.

## **The Fifth Wave: The Absence of Uncomfortable Conversations**

I understand this is a pro-technology summit. We are here to discuss the extraordinary benefits AI can bring. But shouldn't we offset the optimism by also discussing the extraordinary harm it can cause, even on a case-by-case basis?

Not all AI deployments are equally critical. Not every product crosses a risk threshold. But when we are discussing AI deployment in critical sectors, the stakes demand that we spend time having the difficult conversation about what can go wrong.

Yes, these uncomfortable talks might slow down *‘the progress’*, but they would also keep this race to deploy AI across every sector a bit more grounded. They would, by their very nature, make us think harder and provide greater clarity on how urgently we need a regulatory system.

I think we are struggling in that aspect. I'm sure the government recognises the need, but it seems unable or perhaps unwilling to translate that recognition into concrete policy. Maybe it doesn't want to interfere, doesn't want to put the brakes on *‘the progress’*.

But the question I left the summit with is this: will it be too late to put regulations in place once AI is already deeply embedded in critical sectors? Or is *now* the time to build regulatory frameworks alongside the technology, not after it?

## **The Final Wave: Where Do I Belong?**

And now we arrive at the biggest overwhelm of all.

While trying to process the scale of it: lakhs of people, hundreds of expo stalls, endless discussions, nearly everyone radiating super-optimism, I had a moment of silence within.

As a young researcher with deeply mixed feelings about AI (optimistic yet worried) and a desire to build a career in this space... where exactly do I belong?

I know there are camps. Some are vehemently against AI. Some worry about state control. Some worry about market capture. And some are tech enthusiasts who want to shove the technology down everyone's throat.

Do I have a space in this chaos? Will anyone value my views on the need to govern this enormous, fast-moving technology? Or will I be pushed aside by the tech bros and labelled a Luddite?

Is it just me who is scared about my future? Or is it a shared fear among all these tiny fish swimming around me? Are they all internally struggling too, or have they figured out what's in it for them?

If only I could stop each one of them. Shake them by the shoulders. Ask: *What is going on in your head?*

## **Still Swimming**

I don't have a neat conclusion to offer. This isn't that kind of piece.

I walked into the AI Impact Summit as a young AI policy researcher. I walked out still one, but more overwhelmed, more conflicted, and, paradoxically, more determined.

Because here's what I do know: the ocean isn't going to get calmer. The fish aren't going to stop swimming. And someone needs to care about whether we're all heading towards the shore or towards the deep end.

I'm interested to see how governance and regulatory frameworks emerge from all this chaos and noise. Whether we get to the shore. Or whether we drown.

For now, I'm still swimming.
